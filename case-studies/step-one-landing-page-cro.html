01The Problem

Step One runs campaign landing pages for limited-edition colourway drops ("LECs") — short, high-intent campaign pages that send paid and email traffic to a specific product release. These pages are built by me, not handed over by an agency, which means the page structure itself is a variable I can test.

The observation behind the test was a familiar one in DTC: the price lands before the value does. On a campaign landing page, a visitor arriving from a paid ad or an email sees a price figure before they have had any chance to understand what they are buying. For a considered purchase — and underwear is more considered than it looks — that creates sticker shock before desire has been built.

The hypothesis:

If we remove the price from the landing page, there will be less sticker shock, so conversion rate will increase.

This was a deliberately blunt intervention. Not a price change, not a discount, not a payment-plan mechanic — simply removing the number from the above-the-fold experience and letting the value proposition do its work first.

02Method

Platform: Shoplift, landing page test on the LEC experience. Window: 13–15 February 2026, launched alongside the Shredders limited-edition drop. Statistics: two-proportion significance test on conversion rate and add-to-cart rate; zero-inflated test on revenue per visitor. 95% confidence threshold. Guardrail: AOV was tracked explicitly, because removing price visibility is exactly the kind of change that can lift conversion while quietly degrading order value.

The comparison test in this case study is the opposite kind of change: a competitor-focused funnel (AU_CLP_CVR_260127, SO vs Cotton v2) that increased the amount of persuasion on the page. Reading the two together is the point — one removed information, one added it, and the outcomes were dramatically different.

A +42.8% conversion lift at >99.99% confidence — by a wide margin the largest single effect in the entire CRO programme. The variant generated 50 more conversions and A$4,781 more revenue from 203 fewer visitors.

And it did it by moving the whole funnel, not just the last step. Clickthrough rate up 18.4% and add-to-cart rate up 17.0% both reached significance. That's the signature of a page that engages people on arrival: removing the price didn't rescue hesitant shoppers at the checkout, it stopped them bouncing before they had a reason to care.

The honest caveat: AOV fell 5.8% (A$119.54 → A$112.65). Removing price visibility from above the fold did pull in slightly smaller baskets. But RPV still rose 35.6% and revenue rose 28.6%, so the conversion gain dwarfed the basket compression by a wide margin — the trade-off was worth taking, and I'd take it again.

The device split reinforced the pattern: mobile CVR jumped 3.59% → 5.37% and mobile RPV rose A$4.33 → A$5.98, while desktop also improved but more modestly (CVR 3.71% → 4.26%, RPV A$4.12 → A$5.14). Mobile carried the result, which matters because it's where the traffic is.

Rejected. The competitor-focused funnel lost on conversion and on order value — AOV down 13% and RPV down 17.5%. Mobile, which dominates traffic, followed the same pattern: the variant improved clickthrough and add-to-cart slightly, then dropped off at conversion and AOV.

The read: the competitor framing didn't increase persuasion, it added distraction. This is a directional loss on a small sample (~1.8k visitors), and I treated it as such — but the direction was consistent enough across metrics to justify not shipping it.

04Key Insights

1. Removing friction beat adding persuasion. The two tests on the same class of page point in opposite directions. The page that removed information (price) gained 42.8% conversion. The page that added information (competitor comparison) lost 7.1% conversion and 13% AOV. On a high-intent campaign landing page, the visitor has already been persuaded by the ad or email that brought them there — what they need is less to get past, not more to read.

2. The whole funnel moved, and that's what makes it credible. A +42.8% CVR lift alone could be a checkout artefact. But CTR rose 18.4% and ATC rate rose 17.0%, both significant. When a change lifts the top, middle and bottom of the funnel together, the mechanism is clear: people were leaving because of the price, and without it they engaged.

3. The AOV trade-off was real and I took it deliberately. AOV fell 5.8%. I flagged AOV as a guardrail before the test precisely because removing price visibility could suppress basket value — and it did, mildly. RPV rose 35.6% anyway. The discipline is in stating the trade-off rather than presenting the conversion number alone.

4. Small-sample directional losses still earn a decision. The SO vs Cotton test ran on ~1,800 visitors and never reached significance. I still recommended against rollout — because the direction was consistent across conversion, AOV and RPV, with no segment where the variant clearly won. Consistency across independent metrics is evidence even when the sample is thin.

5. Owning the build shortens the loop. I built these pages. That meant the hypothesis could go from observation to live test without a design handoff or a dev queue — and it meant the "remove the price" idea was actually executable, because I understood what the page was doing and why the price was where it was.

05Business Impact

Test Intervention Visitors CVR AOV Revenue in-window Decision
Sticker Shock Removed price 7,595 +42.8% (>99.99% confidence) −5.8% +A$4,781 Shipped
SO vs Cotton v2 Added competitor comparison 1,819 −7.1% −13.0% −A$1,256 Rejected

In plain terms: removing the price from above the fold on a campaign landing page produced a +42.8% conversion rate and +35.6% RPV lift at >99.99% confidence — the largest effect in the CRO programme — generating A$4,781 more revenue from 203 fewer visitors, at a cost of 5.8% on AOV that was comfortably outweighed. The paired test showed that adding competitor-comparison content to the same class of page lost on conversion, AOV and RPV.

Together they established a principle I now apply to every campaign page: on a high-intent landing page, the job is to remove obstacles, not to add argument.

06What I'd Do Differently / Next

  • Test partial price visibility. The variant removed price entirely. The natural next test is price-present-but-de-emphasised (smaller, below the value proposition, or revealed on scroll) to see whether the conversion gain holds while recovering some of the 5.8% AOV loss.
  • Run it on a non-LEC campaign page. This test ran on a limited-edition drop page, where urgency and scarcity were already doing work. Whether the same effect holds on an evergreen campaign page — where the price is the main decision input — is untested and could be very different.
  • Re-run on a larger sample. 7,595 visitors produced a decisive p-value because the effect was enormous, but the AOV question deserves more power than this test had.
  • Test the competitor angle in a lighter form. The full competitor funnel failed, but the earlier reading — that a concise comparison block might work where a full funnel change did not — is still untested. If it's worth revisiting, it's worth doing small.
  • Extend to the PDP. If price above the fold suppresses conversion on a landing page, the same question applies to the product detail page. That's a much larger surface and a much larger prize.
