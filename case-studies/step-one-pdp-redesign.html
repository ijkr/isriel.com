01The Problem

Step One's PDP had three structural problems that no amount of merchandising could fix:

  1. It was slow to navigate between products. Comparing colours or styles meant leaving the page. Customers who wanted to see a second option had to start again.
  2. The pack selector was buried. The mechanism that drives basket value — 3, 5, or 10 packs — was not visually weighted, and the top nav was not being used by customers at all.
  3. Size and fit were off-screen. Larger sizes were invisible on mobile without scrolling, so customers who needed them could not tell they existed.

The hypothesis I carried through the programme:

If we redesign the PDP with a cleaner look and feel along with much quicker product switching, then we will increase conversion rate, because the new design is quicker and more user friendly enabling users to browse and ATC more efficiently.

The design concept was "Islands" — modular content blocks that persist in the viewport, with a stepped selection process (fit → size → pack) and a pop-out colour selector showing larger product images. The critical build decision was that USPs and the pack selector stay in view while the customer browses, so the value proposition never scrolls away.

02Method

Platform: Shoplift, storefront theme tests. Statistics: two-proportion z-test for CVR and ATC rate; zero-inflated z-test for RPV. α = 0.05. Segmentation every read: device (mobile is ~85% of traffic), new vs returning, channel. Diagnostic layer: SKU-prefix unit mix and pack-tier analysis, to explain why a metric moved.

I ran this as an iterative programme, not a single test. Each round kept what worked and rebuilt what didn't — and one round was killed outright rather than reported.

This is the classic trap, and I did not ship it. The variant produced more orders (1,449 vs 1,419) at a marginally better conversion rate — and A$9,151 less revenue, because average order value fell 6.8%. More, smaller baskets.

The read: the Islands layout improved browsing and cart entry but degraded the pack-selection step. Customers were reaching cart with shallower packs. A conversion win that destroys order value is a loss, and I reported it as one.

Iteration 2 — AU_Theme_CVR_260116 | Islands, Most PDPs | 16–19 Jan 2026

Killed. The experiment log records: "Bugged Quick Add - needs to be retested." Status inconclusive, result declared invalid rather than reported. A quick-add bug in the roll-out to broader PDPs made the data unusable, so I stopped it and fixed the build.

The important detail is what moved and what didn't. CVR jumped 17.6% and revenue 18.8%, but ATC rate was flat (+0.7%). The redesign did not push more people into the cart — it made the people already in the funnel convert. AOV was also flat, so the entire gain was clean: more orders at the same value, not the same orders at inflated value.

Device segmentation held the pattern:

Unlike AU Round 4, mobile now carried the gain — the volume driver, at ~87% of traffic, moved with desktop rather than lagging it. That was the fix the AU read had demanded.

Iteration 5 — AU_Theme_CVR_260526 | PDP v3 | from 26 May 2026

With the UK confirmed, AU went back for a v3 targeting the elements the earlier rounds had exposed. The build included:

  • Stepped process — select fit, then size, then pack, as an explicit sequence, because customers were not using the top nav
  • USPs pinned to the image gallery and persisting in the viewport
  • Reversed pack-button hierarchy — weighting higher-value packs first, to fix the shallow-basket problem iteration 1 had revealed
  • Integrated bundle builder with a pop-out colour selector for larger product images

The hypothesis states the goal directly: "stepped process — allows users to select their preferred fit easily (people weren't using the top nav), reversed pack buttons to focus on higher value packs." This is the round that attacks the AOV leak at its source.

Iteration 6 — AU_Theme_CVR_260616 | PDP v3, Paid Search Target | from 16 Jun 2026

A segment-targeted follow-up, and the detail I'd point to as the mark of a mature programme. The experiment log reads:

"Continues from AU_Theme_CVR_260526 - specifically targets paid as this channel suffered during the last test."

Rather than accepting a global result or abandoning a channel, I isolated paid search — the channel that regressed — into its own conditional test. That is the difference between running tests and running a programme.

04Key Insights

1. A conversion win that lowers AOV is a loss. Iteration 1 delivered more orders and a better CVR and A$9,151 less revenue. If I had reported CVR as the headline, it would have read as a win. The Islands layout was genuinely improving the browsing experience while quietly degrading the pack-selection step — and only the revenue view exposed it. This is why I treat RPV, not CVR, as the commercial guardrail even on a CVR-primary test.

2. The gap between ATC and CVR tells you which half of the funnel you fixed. Iteration 4 lifted CVR 17.6% with flat ATC rate. The redesign was not adding people to the cart — it was converting the people already there. That distinction changes the follow-up: you don't need more traffic, you need the same traffic better served.

3. Desktop and mobile fail for different reasons, and you have to read them separately. AU Round 4 was desktop-only in effect (RPV +28.8% desktop vs −2.0% mobile). The UK version fixed it — mobile moved +18.8% CVR. Had I read only the aggregate in January, I would have missed that the layout's persistent-USP advantage depends on viewport, and shipped something that helped 15% of traffic and hurt the rest.

4. Killing a test is a result. Iteration 2 was stopped on a quick-add bug and its data declared invalid rather than published with a caveat. A bugged test reported as a directional result contaminates every decision built on it.

5. Replication is a separate test, not a formality. The same design shipped +2.7% RPV in AU and +19.8% RPV in the UK. UK traffic skews different, the price points differ (£15–17 vs A$29–33), and the mobile/desktop mix differs. The UK result is not confirmation of the AU result — it is a stronger, separate finding that happened to agree.

In plain terms: across six iterations and two markets I rebuilt the product detail page from a static template into a stepped, modular experience with persistent value messaging and an in-viewport pack selector. The UK rollout delivered a statistically definitive +17.6% conversion rate and +18.8% revenue uplift (>99.99% confidence) with flat AOV — a clean, non-inflated gain of £7,243 in a six-day window on 31,587 visitors.

The programme's more important output was the discipline it demonstrated: I caught and refused a conversion "win" that was costing A$9,151 in revenue, killed a bugged test rather than reporting it, diagnosed the device split that explained why the first layout failed on mobile, and then built a follow-up that targets the single channel that regressed.

06What I'd Do Differently / Next

  • Instrument AOV as a co-primary on any layout test, not a secondary. Iteration 1's problem was invisible in the CVR headline. A layout change that touches the pack selector should never be evaluated on conversion alone.
  • Run the AU Round 4 test longer. Three days and 7,841 visitors gave a +5.1% CVR signal at 93.2% confidence — close, but not conclusive. The UK result later validated the direction, but I would have pre-committed a longer window.
  • Fix mobile in the same round as desktop. AU Round 4 shipped a desktop-favouring layout and needed the UK round to prove mobile could work. Reading the device split before the next build would have saved a cycle.
  • Test the pack-button hierarchy in isolation. The reversed hierarchy is currently bundled into v3 alongside the stepped process and the bundle builder, so if v3 wins I won't know which element earned it. A cleaner design would have split the hierarchy change into its own test — it is the direct fix for the iteration-1 AOV leak and deserves its own attribution.
  • Close the paid-search question. AU_Theme_CVR_260616 is the right follow-up; the priority is getting it to significance so the channel decision is settled rather than parked.
