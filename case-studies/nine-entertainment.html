01The Problem

Nine's mastheads carry four different audiences and one shared commercial problem: converting readers into paying subscribers without damaging the value of the subscription itself. The Sydney Morning Herald, The Age and the AFR each sit behind a paywall with a different content value proposition and a different price sensitivity, and the Brisbane Times adds a fourth register.

Two structural issues sat underneath the day-to-day testing:

  1. Acquisition pricing was not calibrated to the market. SMH and The Age were the only publishers not widely advertising a discounted introductory offer, and the AFR's intro offer was the most expensive in the business and finance market against international competitors.
  2. Promotion-acquired subscribers were not surviving the end of their promotion. 26% of subscribers cancelled as soon as their promotion ended, and only 46% remained after their first year.

The instruction behind the programme was to stop treating the paywall as a single lever. Pricing, offer placement, renewal mechanics and the trust signals around the offer are separate variables, tested against named audiences on the Piano platform.

02Method

Platform: Piano — A/B and XT testing across paywall, registration and homepage surfaces. Primary metrics: conversion rate, clickthrough rate, incremental subscriptions, annualised revenue. Statistical threshold: win probability, with 95%+ required for implementation. Analysis depth: audience segments (anonymous, registered, direct), device-level splits, and metered vs subscriber-only paywall variants. Prioritisation: a framework scoring every hypothesis by impact × complexity × campaign alignment, built from stakeholder input and coordinated with Piano and product for build. Reporting: campaign performance at a glance, test-level deep dives, and a programme-level summary to Subscriptions & Marketing and the Product teams.

The programme also ran a bi-monthly working session with Piano on platform best practice and targeting capability, so that capability discovered in one test became available to the next one.

03The Programmes

Programme 1 — Intro offer pricing | Nov–Dec 2023 flash sale

The challenge. SMH and The Age were the only publishers not advertising a discounted intro offer widely in market, and AFR's intro offer was the most expensive in the business and finance market against international competitors.

The approach. A multi-channel test — paywall, email and landing page — each measured independently before the winning combination was coordinated.

Email channel — four offers tested against 20% of the audience, with the winner sent to the remaining 80%:

Offer Result
$1/week for 6 months Winner — +12% conversion uplift against the other offers
6 months for the price of 2 +225% uplift — but the lowest retention (free-trial pattern)
$79 for 1 year +225% uplift on other offers

Paywall channel — five price points tested across metered and subscriber-only paywalls:

Price point Paywall type Result
$5/week for 3 months AFR, subscriber-only +47% conversion, 100% win probability, $6K incremental revenue
$1/week for 3 months Metered +129% conversion, $26K annualised (27 incremental units)
$12 for 3 months Metered +15% conversion — best for The Age audience

Programme impact: 2,751 subscriptions acquired in total — 75% of the SMH/The Age target and 83% of the AFR target.

Key insight. Different price points win on different paywall types. Metered paywalls respond to deep discounting; subscriber-only paywalls respond to moderate offers. The second finding was organisational rather than statistical: the winning email offer and the paywall offer have to be aligned, or the two channels contradict each other in front of the same reader.

Programme 2 — Personalised pricing and retention | FY22–FY24, all mastheads

The challenge. 26% of subscribers cancel the moment their promotion ends, only 46% remain after year one, and price sensitivity among promotion-acquired subscribers peaks exactly at renewal.

The approach. A churn propensity model scoring each subscriber 1–10, segmenting at-risk subscribers into three risk tiers and treating each differently at renewal:

Risk tier Score Renewal treatment
Low 1–4 Full price renewal
Intermediate 5–7 Stepped mid-level discount
High 8–10 Extended, stronger discount to retain longer

Transactional eDM and SMS comms went out at D-6 before renewal carrying the personalised discount, so the offer arrived before the cancellation decision rather than after it.

Modelled outcome. If 30–40% of no-engagement and low-engagement subscribers are saved, the model retains an additional 14,600–20,000 subscribers — a projected $400,000–$530,000 in additional annual revenue.

The pivot that matters. With 5–10% precision issues in the propensity model, the decision was to stop relying on it as a targeting engine and move to randomised A/B testing on flash-sale cohorts instead. A model with a 5–10% precision problem is a bad basis for a pricing decision; randomisation answers the same question without that exposure.

Key insight. Retention pricing is more cost-effective than acquisition — retaining a subscriber at a 50–79% discount beats losing the full-price revenue entirely. It also has to be segment-specific: Starter Digital and Premium Digital have genuinely different churn patterns, and a single renewal funnel cannot serve both.

Programme 3 — Social ribbon: removing the 30-second timer | AFR, CRO-30.0-2312

The challenge. The social ribbon carried a 30-second auto-dismissing timer, which capped how long a reader could read and act on the offer, and required them to act to dismiss it. The urgency pressure was plausibly costing engagement rather than creating it.

The change. One variable: the timer was removed so the ribbon persists until the reader actively dismisses it.

Metric Result
Conversion +33% lift
Clickthrough rate +26% lift — 100% winning probability, 293 incremental clicks
Incremental sales 3
Annualised incremental revenue $6K
Test scale 21 days, 947K visitors

Key insight. Removing artificial expiry friction increased engagement rather than reducing urgency. Recommendation carried into the next template build for the social ribbon.

Programme 4 — Multi-package paywall | SMH & The Age, CRO-73/74.0-2404

The challenge. A single-package paywall underperformed. Readers were not offered enough choice to find their own price point, and the paywall presented one price as take-it-or-leave-it.

The change. The single-package paywall was replaced with a multi-package paywall showing three pricing options simultaneously.

04What the Programme Taught

Across 68 tests, the pattern in the winners was consistent enough to change how the next year's hypotheses were written:

  • Restricting access beat improving access. Tests that reduced access to content were more likely to win at confidence than tests that added or improved it.
  • Alternate price offers won more often than functionality or behaviour changes — pricing changes reached confidence at a higher rate than mechanical ones.
  • Copy + CTA beat copy-only and CTA-only. Combined messaging and action changes were both more likely to win at confidence and more likely to generate the largest volume of incremental subscriptions.
  • Design changes that eased navigation to the desired action drove most of the subscriptions — the wins clustered where the path to the action got shorter, not where the page got prettier.

In plain terms: a full experimentation stack across four mastheads, with 68 tests run in a single financial year, conclusive wins on intro-offer pricing, paywall structure and social-ribbon friction, and a personalised retention-pricing strategy modelled at $400K–$530K in annual revenue. The programme produced 1,391 incremental subscriptions from conclusive winners alone, against a 63% win rate.

06What I'd Do Differently / Next

  • Fix the propensity model's precision before shipping it as targeting. A 5–10% precision issue was caught and the programme correctly pivoted to randomised testing — but probabilistic scoring should have been validated against a randomised hold-out from the start.
  • Read the retention offers as experiments, not as margin decisions. The 50–79% retention discounts were priced commercially; they should have carried their own randomised structure so the discount depth itself was a tested variable.
  • Align email and paywall offers by default. The intro-offer programme proved the alignment matters, and it should have been a standing rule rather than a finding.
  • Attribute copy + CTA separately more often. The programme-level pattern was clear, but at test level the two were usually bundled — which is the same attribution problem I fix on any programme.
